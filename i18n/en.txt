About these distances

is a typical sailing distance between the two ports. Where no figure exists for the pair directly, it is built from legs through other ports, so it may be longer than a direct passage.
Straight-line distance

Great circle vs rhumb line
Great circle
the shortest path between two points on the globe. The course changes continuously along the way (except along a meridian or the equator).
Rhumb line
(loxodrome): a path that crosses every meridian at the same angle, so you can steer one constant course. It appears as a straight line on a Mercator chart but is longer than the great circle — the difference grows with distance and latitude, and is small for short coastal passages.
Δψ = ln( tan(π/4 + φ2/2) / tan(π/4 + φ1/2) ) q = Δφ / Δψ (or cos φ on an E–W course) d = √(Δφ² + q²·Δλ²) · R θ = atan2(Δλ, Δψ)

Sunrise/sunset = upper limb of the Sun on the horizon, i.e. Sun's centre at −0.833° (34′ refraction + 16′ semi-diameter), solved iteratively with the NOAA solar equations. Civil twilight: −6°. Nautical twilight: −12°.
Accuracy is about ±1 minute between latitudes 72° N and 72° S; actual refraction depends on weather. Near the poles the Sun may not rise or set — MEIKO reports polar night or midnight sun instead of a time.

Compass calibration & accuracy
True vs magnetic:
the Qibla bearing shown is from true (geographic) north. A magnetic compass differs by the local magnetic variation. The live compass corrects for it with the World Magnetic Model 2025, or with the value you enter above.
Calibrate:
move the phone slowly in a figure-8 pattern several times, then hold it flat (screen up), away from your body.
Interference:
metal objects, magnets, phone cases with magnets, speakers, vehicles and especially
can deflect the sensor by many degrees. On board, prefer the ship's gyro/true heading: steer or face the true bearing shown.
MEIKO cannot tell whether your sensor is calibrated. Treat the live compass as approximate; the calculated bearing is the reliable figure.
Without a compass sensor you can still use the bearing: e.g. with the Sun's azimuth, a known landmark, or a properly corrected compass.

— a Signal K server or Wi-Fi NMEA gateway that offers a WebSocket. Signal K JSON and raw NMEA lines are both understood.

How to read the chart
The circle is the whole sky: the centre is straight overhead (zenith), the edge is the horizon. Hold the phone above your head with
toward north — east is on the left, as when looking up. Rings mark 30° and 60° altitude.
Navigational stars between about 15° and 70° altitude are the best for sights (shown bold in the table). Bright planets and the Moon are
Star positions are computed (Hipparcos catalogue, precessed to date; nutation and aberration ignored, ~1′). Planets and the Moon are not identified. A position from stars is a backup estimate — confirm with every other means available.
Take or choose a photo of the night sky. MEIKO finds the stars, matches them to its offline catalogue and shows the constellations, which way the camera was pointing and where the celestial pole is. If the sea horizon is in the picture, it can also estimate your position.

How it works & how accurate it is
Identification.
MEIKO finds point-like bright spots in the picture, then compares the shapes of triangles between the brightest ones with triangles between catalogue stars (shape does not depend on zoom or rotation). A match is accepted only if many other catalogue stars then land on stars in the photo, far more than chance would give. If a match is uncertain, MEIKO says so instead of guessing. Planets, the Moon, aircraft and satellites are not in the catalogue and are ignored.
Direction.
The celestial poles are fixed in the sky. The horizon point directly below the north celestial pole (near Polaris) is true north; below the south celestial pole (found from Gubuk Penceng / Crux) is true south. This needs no time or position. With the time and an approximate position MEIKO also gives the camera's compass bearing.
Position.
Marking the sea horizon turns every identified star into a sextant-like altitude, corrected for dip and refraction, and solved by least squares — the same method as a star fix. But a phone photo is far less precise than a sextant: one pixel is several minutes of arc and each minute is one nautical mile, and lens distortion and a tilted horizon line add more. Expect
tens of nautical miles
of error, more with few stars or a hazy horizon. It is a rough check when GPS is lost, not a navigational fix. Time matters too: a clock error of 1 minute moves the position 15′ of longitude.

GHA and declination of each star are computed from the Hipparcos catalogue (precessed to the date, GMST from IAU 1982), good to about 1′ — check against the Nautical Almanac when you have it. Refraction: Bennett, with temperature and pressure. The fix is the least-squares point of all lines of position (no running fix: enter sights taken within a few minutes, or advance the DR yourself).

How it works and privacy
Public fleet.
Everyone signed in to MEIKO joins it automatically, so vessels that are online see each other without a code. Anyone using MEIKO can see the nickname, vessel and (only if you share it) position of the vessels in it. Your position is shared by default; switch
off to hide it.
General Group
is one chat room for everyone, no code: join it under Chat. It is not private and not moderated; tap a name to
Block
Report
a vessel.
MEIKO has no server of its own. Messages travel through free public
Nostr relays
encrypted, so the relays see only scrambled data. In a
private group
the key is made from the group code: anyone who knows the code can read the group, so share it only with people you trust. The public fleet's key is given only to signed-in MEIKO accounts (it is not in the app's code), so people without a MEIKO account cannot read positions or General Group. Anyone who creates a MEIKO account can.
Your position is sent only while
is on, every 30 seconds, and only while MEIKO is open. Phones pause web apps in the background, so others may see you as offline.
Private chat
tap a vessel on the map or in the list, then
No extra code is needed. It is encrypted a second time with a key only your device and theirs can compute, so nobody else, including other vessels in the fleet, can read it. For very sensitive information you can optionally compare the number under
Verify
by VHF or phone. Relays and group members can still see
you sent a private message and when, but not to whom or what it says. Anyone who gets hold of an unlocked phone can read its messages.
Positions are not stored by the relays; recent chat (up to 2 days) is, so people who join later can read it. The last 100 messages are also kept on this device.
Photos and files
(the paperclip) are encrypted like messages (private ones end-to-end) and sent in small parts of 16 KB, because public relays refuse large messages. Photos are compressed to about 300 KB; other files can be up to 512 KB. A 300 KB photo takes about 20 parts and roughly 6–10 seconds; keep MEIKO open until it says sent. Some relays limit how fast you may send, so a large file can arrive incomplete. Received files are kept in this browser's storage for 3 days.
Vessels show as online if they sent something in the last 3 minutes; otherwise you see when they were last seen.
